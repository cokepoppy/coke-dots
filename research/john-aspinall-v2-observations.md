## Replication implications

1. Keep the first-run computer choice before the welcome conversation. The first card is selected by default; the local-computer row has its own enabled switch. The screen says this choice can change later. The exact first-card detail is too blurred to transcribe; map the option only to a capability we can verify. Coke Dots stores the choice by tenant and exposes it again in profile settings. Its local service uses a tenant-isolated Chrome profile and does not imply macOS file or app access.
2. After the 01:45 setup transition, show the observed transient “The computer is connected to your dot” status while entering the welcome conversation. The five-second dismissal is a Coke Dots implementation choice; the video only bounds it between sampled frames. Preserve the separation between the dot conversation, its details/activity side panel, and the distinct computer viewport. In observed computer states, status text identifies who currently controls the computer and a “Take over” action is visible.
3. The video shows a pill-style “Customize your dot” action during first-run conversation at about 01:55–01:59, followed by the Color/Characters/Pets editor at 02:00–02:39. A separate Shape/Eyes/Glasses/Accessories editor appears at 02:46 and 07:30–07:41. Both editors have a live preview and Save. Tiny option names, pet behavior, and fresh-session persistence are unverified.
4. Keep the observed first-run copy exact: the name question is “Want to give me a name?” The inspected frames do not show the user naming the Dot in that part of setup; the later conversation is a different state. Do not insert a fabricated user reply or a Dot confirmation into the initial transcript.
5. Represent task proposals, reported in-progress work, completed outputs, and user-visible notifications as different states. This video shows all four concepts being discussed or displayed, but it also includes a reported missed notification and does not independently establish a schedule run.
6. Keep Slack setup unimplemented until a modal is directly observed; the prior note that such a modal appeared at 07:12 was incorrect. The side panel shows a Slack action, but not a completed connection flow in the retained captures.
7. Treat the Amazon failure as a required negative test lead because the chapter and presenter description call it out. Revisit the source footage frame-by-frame before implementing a specific error screen; that failure UI was not visible in the samples saved from this review.
8. Full Take over → user action → Return control, scheduled task creation/editing, notification delivery, and task resume after app restart remain open replay scenarios. Do not mark them evidenced by this video review.
9. Pixel-level comparison remains pending. Obtain a clean, stable source frame and known viewport before measuring or claiming visual parity.
